\begin{abstract}
Blockchain systems derive their integrity guarantees from elliptic-curve and
factorisation-based cryptography, both of which Shor's algorithm reduces to
polynomial time. Because distributed ledgers retain public keys and signatures
indefinitely, their exposure is cumulative rather than transient: material
committed today remains available to a future quantum adversary. Organisations
preparing for this transition first need an inventory of where vulnerable
primitives are actually invoked, yet established smart-contract analysers target
logical defects and do not report cryptographic composition. We present a
toolkit that locates quantum-vulnerable call sites in Solidity, Python,
JavaScript and Go sources, maps each to its NIST-standardised replacement,
exports a CycloneDX cryptographic bill of materials, generates regulator-facing
documentation, and anchors a tamper-evident digest of each assessment on a
public ledger. Our central technical claim concerns precision rather than
coverage. We show that a naive lexical detector of this kind is dominated by two
systematic error sources: matches inside comments and string literals, and
multiple patterns firing on a single call site. We introduce offset-preserving
non-code masking and per-line family deduplication to eliminate both, and a
three-term exposure score that replaces a severity sum which we demonstrate
saturates for any file containing four or more high-severity findings. On a
hand-labelled benchmark of seven artefacts containing nineteen annotated call
sites, of which three artefacts are deliberate hard negatives, these mechanisms
raise precision from 0.633 to 1.000 at unchanged recall. We report this as an
ablation establishing that each mechanism removes the error class it targets,
and we state explicitly why the absolute figures do not constitute an
independent accuracy estimate. We additionally describe a dual-runtime
implementation, verified to produce identical findings in both runtimes, that
permits analysis of proprietary source without transmitting it.

\keywords{Post-quantum cryptography \and Blockchain security \and Static
analysis \and ML-DSA \and ML-KEM \and Cryptographic bill of materials \and
Regulatory compliance.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Public blockchains rest on a narrow cryptographic base. Ethereum and Bitcoin
authenticate transactions with ECDSA over the secp256k1 curve; smart contracts
commonly verify delegated authorisation through the \texttt{ecrecover}
precompile; off-chain components that surround a deployment typically use RSA
for key transport and SHA-256 for commitment. Each of these rests on a problem
that a sufficiently capable quantum computer solves efficiently. Shor's
algorithm~\cite{shor1997} yields polynomial-time integer factorisation and
discrete logarithms, which breaks RSA and ECDSA outright. Grover's
algorithm~\cite{grover1996} provides a quadratic speedup for unstructured
search, which halves the effective security margin of symmetric primitives and
hash functions, leaving AES-128 at roughly 64 bits and SHA-256 at roughly 128
bits of preimage resistance.

Distributed ledgers occupy an unusually exposed position in this transition. In
most systems a cryptographic weakness is bounded in time: once a vulnerable
channel is retired, traffic that passed through it is no longer available to an
attacker. A public ledger inverts this property. Every public key and every
signature it has ever recorded remains permanently retrievable, so an adversary
who acquires quantum capability at some future point can operate
retrospectively against the entire history. Mosca's framing of the migration
deadline~\cite{mosca2018} makes the consequence concrete: when the required
secrecy lifetime plus the migration time exceeds the time until a
cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists, the organisation is already
late. For an immutable ledger the secrecy lifetime is unbounded, which collapses
the inequality and makes the deadline immediate in principle.

Standardisation has removed the main technical obstacle to acting. In August
2024 NIST finalised FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, module-lattice key
encapsulation)~\cite{fips203}, FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, module-lattice
signatures)~\cite{fips204} and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, stateless hash-based
signatures)~\cite{fips205}. NIST's subsequent transition
guidance~\cite{nistir8547} sets out a schedule under which the classical
primitives in question are deprecated and then disallowed. In India, the Reserve
Bank of India's cybersecurity framework and CERT-In's directions place
corresponding obligations on regulated entities to maintain awareness of
cryptographic exposure.

What remains unaddressed is the first operational step. Before an organisation
can migrate, it must know where the vulnerable primitives are invoked, with
enough specificity to assign the work. This is an inventory problem, and it is
distinct from the vulnerability classes that existing smart-contract tooling
addresses. Slither~\cite{slither}, Mythril and Manticore detect reentrancy,
arithmetic overflow and access-control defects. They do not report which
cryptographic algorithms a codebase composes, because that has not historically
been a defect. CryptoGuard~\cite{cryptoguard} does analyse cryptographic API
usage, but targets misuse rules in Java rather than quantum exposure in ledger
code.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

We address the following problem. Given a source artefact drawn from a
blockchain deployment, produce a list of the locations at which
quantum-vulnerable cryptographic primitives are invoked, such that the list is
specific enough to drive a migration and precise enough that an engineer
reviewing it does not lose confidence in the tool.

The precision requirement is the substantive one, and it is easy to
underestimate. A detector of this kind is normally built from a pattern set
matched against source text. Such a detector is straightforward to make
complete, in the sense of finding every genuine call site, and surprisingly
difficult to make precise. Two error sources dominate. Cryptographic algorithm
names occur heavily in comments, documentation strings and literals,
particularly in code that is being migrated, where the prose discusses exactly
the primitives that have been removed. And a single call site is frequently
matched by several patterns at once, so the naive finding count exceeds the
number of locations an engineer must actually visit. Both errors inflate every
downstream number, including the risk score and the remediation estimate.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A precision-oriented detection method for quantum-vulnerable
  cryptography in source code, combining offset-preserving non-code masking with
  per-line family deduplication. Masking blanks comments and string literals
  while preserving byte offsets and newlines, so reported line numbers remain
  exact; deduplication collapses redundant matches so that one call site yields
  one finding (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

  \item An exposure score that does not saturate. We show that the severity-sum
  scoring used in the preliminary version of this system assigns the maximum
  value to any artefact containing four or more high-severity findings, and
  report that it produced a mean score of 99.5 across the scan records
  accumulated during development. We replace it with a three-term model over
  worst severity, family breadth and finding density
  (Section~\ref{sec:scoring}).

  \item A hand-labelled benchmark of seven artefacts and nineteen annotated call
  sites, in which three artefacts are zero-label hard negatives: a
  post-quantum implementation, a module whose vulnerable primitives appear only
  in prose, and a module of identifiers that merely contain primitive names as
  substrings. The benchmark and its labelling policy are released with the
  implementation (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}).

  \item An ablation over three detector configurations quantifying the
  contribution of each mechanism, together with an explicit account of why the
  absolute scores overstate field accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

  \item A dual-runtime implementation --- a Python reference and a browser port
  --- verified to produce identical findings, counts, risk scores and
  post-quantum detections across the full benchmark. The browser runtime
  permits assessment of proprietary source that an organisation will not
  transmit (Section~\ref{sec:dualruntime}).

  \item An integration path from detection to governance: CycloneDX
  cryptographic bill of materials export, regulator-facing report generation,
  and non-custodial ledger anchoring of assessment digests
  (Sections~\ref{sec:cbom} and~\ref{sec:anchor}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Quantum Threats to Deployed Primitives}

Classical public-key cryptography rests on problems believed intractable for
classical computers. RSA depends on integer factorisation; ECDSA on the
elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem; Diffie--Hellman on the discrete
logarithm problem in a finite field. Shor's algorithm~\cite{shor1997} solves all
three in polynomial time on a quantum computer, so these primitives are broken
rather than weakened. The practical question is only when suitable hardware
exists. Webber et al.~\cite{webber2022} estimate that recovering a Bitcoin
private key within one hour would require on the order of hundreds of millions
of physical qubits under plausible error-correction assumptions, which is far
beyond present capability; however, resource estimates have fallen repeatedly as
error-correction techniques have improved, and the relevant planning horizon is
set by regulators rather than by any single estimate.

Grover's algorithm~\cite{grover1996} acts differently. It confers a quadratic
speedup on unstructured search, which halves effective security for symmetric
constructions. This weakens rather than destroys: AES-128 retains roughly 64-bit
security and SHA-256 roughly 128-bit preimage resistance against a quantum
adversary. The standard response is to lengthen parameters, moving to AES-256
and SHA-384 or SHA-512, rather than to change the construction.

\subsection{Standardised Replacements}

Table~\ref{tab:nist} lists the finalised NIST standards that form the target of
any migration. Bernstein and Lange~\cite{bernstein2017} survey the underlying
mathematics.

\caption{Finalised NIST post-quantum standards and the classical primitives they

\label{tab:nist}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Standard} & \textbf{Algorithm} & \textbf{Hardness basis} & \textbf{Replaces} \\
\midrule
FIPS 203 & ML-KEM (Kyber)      & Module lattices & RSA transport, DH, ECDH \\
FIPS 204 & ML-DSA (Dilithium)  & Module lattices & RSA signatures, ECDSA \\

A practical consideration shapes our recommendations for on-chain code. ML-DSA
signatures and public keys are substantially larger than their ECDSA
counterparts, and lattice verification is not expressible within current
Ethereum gas limits at acceptable cost. Consequently the appropriate migration
for a contract that verifies signatures on-chain is not a drop-in substitution
but an architectural change: verification moves off-chain and the contract
records a commitment to the verified result. Our migration output states this
rather than offering a substitution that cannot be deployed. A second constraint
applies to Keccak-256: because Ethereum address derivation depends on it,
replacing it inside a deployed contract is not possible without breaking
identity, so we scope hash recommendations to off-chain use.

\subsection{Static Analysis for Blockchain and Cryptography}

Smart-contract analysis is a mature area. Slither~\cite{slither} performs
static analysis over an intermediate representation of Solidity and detects a
broad catalogue of logical vulnerability patterns; Mythril applies symbolic
execution to EVM bytecode; Manticore supports symbolic exploration of both. None
reports cryptographic composition, because an ECDSA verification is
well-formed code under their threat models.

Cryptographic-misuse detection has been studied separately.
CryptoGuard~\cite{cryptoguard} identifies misuse patterns such as hard-coded
keys and insecure modes in Java applications with low false-positive rates, and
CrySL~\cite{crysl} provides a specification language for correct API usage.
These target \emph{incorrect use of sound primitives}. Our problem is the
complement: correct use of primitives that will become unsound. The two are not
interchangeable, and a misuse detector reports nothing for a textbook-correct
ECDSA implementation, which is precisely the case of interest here.

The inventory framing has recently been formalised through the cryptographic
bill of materials. CycloneDX~\cite{cyclonedx} added a
\texttt{cryptographic-asset} component type that allows an algorithm, its
primitive class and its execution environment to be expressed in a
machine-readable inventory. Emitting this format rather than a bespoke one
situates our output within existing supply-chain tooling, and we adopt it for
that reason.

To our knowledge no published tool combines quantum-vulnerability detection for
ledger code, standards-aligned migration planning, cryptographic inventory
export, and verifiable assessment records. The gap we address is therefore
partly integrative. The methodological contribution, however, is the precision
treatment in Section~\ref{sec:method}, which applies to any lexical
cryptographic inventory tool.

\section{Threat Model and Design Goals}
\label{sec:threat}

We state the adversary and the scope precisely, because the scope boundary
explains several design decisions and all of the limitations.

\paragraph{Adversary.} We assume a \emph{harvest-now, decrypt-later} adversary
who records public ledger data continuously and acquires cryptographically
relevant quantum capability at an unknown future time, after which it can
recover private keys from recorded public keys and forge signatures. The
adversary does not need access to the organisation's systems, because the
material it requires is already public.

\paragraph{Scope.} The tool answers the question: \emph{at which source
locations does this artefact invoke a primitive that the above adversary
defeats?} It does not evaluate protocol design, key management, randomness
quality, or whether a correctly named primitive is correctly used. These are
separate analyses, and we are explicit that our output is a migration worklist
rather than a cryptographic audit.

\paragraph{Design goals.} Four goals follow.
\textbf{G1, precision over volume}: an engineer must be able to trust that each
reported location requires work, because a worklist that reports documentation
as a defect is discarded on first review.
\textbf{G2, actionable specificity}: each finding must carry an exact line and a
named replacement with its governing standard.
\textbf{G3, confidentiality}: assessing proprietary ledger code must not require
transmitting it, since the organisations most exposed are least able to upload
source to a third party.
\textbf{G4, verifiability}: an assessment presented to a regulator or insurer
must be independently checkable for integrity and date, without trusting the
assessing party.

\paragraph{Stage 1, masking and matching.} The artefact is normalised so that
only executable text is searched, then matched against a pattern set covering
five algorithm families. Section~\ref{sec:masking} describes the masking step.

\paragraph{Stage 2, deduplication.} Redundant matches on the same line and
family are collapsed, and names found only in masked regions are retained
separately as informational context rather than discarded, so a reviewer can see
what the tool chose not to count.

\paragraph{Stage 3, scoring.} The surviving findings are reduced to a single
exposure score by the model in Section~\ref{sec:scoring}.

\paragraph{Stage 4, migration planning.} Findings are grouped by family and each
group is mapped to its replacement, governing standard, affected lines and a
language-appropriate code template. The planner emits a Solidity template where
the artefact is Solidity and the family admits an on-chain treatment, and
otherwise emits the off-chain pattern discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:background}.

\paragraph{Stage 5, governance artefacts.} Three outputs are derived: a
CycloneDX inventory (Section~\ref{sec:cbom}), a report rendered against a
regulator-specific template, and a ledger-anchored digest
(Section~\ref{sec:anchor}).

\section{Detection Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Pattern Set}

We cover five families (Table~\ref{tab:families}). Each pattern targets an
invocation or import form rather than a bare algorithm name, which is the first
and weakest precision measure; the mechanisms in the following subsections do
the substantive work.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Algorithm families, severity assignment and replacement target.
Severity reflects whether the primitive is broken (HIGH) or weakened (MEDIUM)
by the adversary of Section~\ref{sec:threat}.}
\label{tab:families}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lclp{32mm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Family} & \textbf{Sev.} & \textbf{Replacement} & \textbf{Representative pattern} \\
\midrule
ECDSA      & HIGH   & ML-DSA (FIPS 204)        & \texttt{ecrecover(}, \texttt{secp256k1} \\
RSA        & HIGH   & ML-KEM (FIPS 203)        & \texttt{RSA.}, \texttt{Crypto.PublicKey} import \\
DH         & HIGH   & ML-KEM (FIPS 203)        & \texttt{diffie\_hellman}, \texttt{ECDH} \\
SHA256     & MEDIUM & SHA-384/512 (FIPS 180-4) & \texttt{hashlib.sha256(}, \texttt{keccak256(} \\
AES\_SHORT & MEDIUM & AES-256-GCM (FIPS 197)   & \texttt{AES.new(...MODE\_}, \texttt{AES-128} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

A design note on word boundaries. The preliminary pattern set included
unanchored fragments, of which a bare \texttt{dh} followed by optional
whitespace and a period was the clearest error: it matches any identifier ending
in those characters, so ordinary application code containing \texttt{bandwidth}
or \texttt{width} produced spurious key-exchange findings. Anchoring patterns at
word boundaries removes this class. The converse error is equally real and
arises from over-anchoring: a trailing boundary on \texttt{dilithium} prevents
matching \texttt{dilithium2}, and on \texttt{diffie\_hellman} prevents matching
\texttt{diffie\_hellman\_exchange}, because the following character is a word
character in both cases. Because real library and function names carry parameter
suffixes, boundary placement must be asymmetric: leading boundary, no trailing
boundary, for any name that admits a suffix. Both faults were present in our own
preliminary implementation and were identified by the benchmark of
Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}.

\subsection{Offset-Preserving Non-Code Masking}
\label{sec:masking}

Algorithm names appear in comments and string literals at high density, and the
density is highest in exactly the codebases of interest: a module undergoing
migration documents the primitives it has removed. A detector that matches raw
text therefore reports migration documentation as unmigrated code, which is both
wrong and conspicuous to a reviewer.

The obvious remedy, deleting comments and strings before matching, destroys the
mapping from match offset to source line, and line numbers are the output's
principal value. We instead \emph{mask}: every character inside a comment or
string literal is replaced by a space, while newlines are preserved. The
resulting buffer has identical length and identical line structure to the
original, so an offset in the masked buffer indexes the original source
directly, and matches are confined to executable text.

Alternation order matters. Triple-quoted strings and block comments must be
consumed before single-line forms, or a line comment inside a documentation
string terminates matching prematurely. Single-quoted and double-quoted literal
patterns exclude newlines, which bounds the damage from an unbalanced quote to
one line rather than allowing it to swallow the remainder of the file. We note
that this is lexical approximation, not parsing: the construction is
language-agnostic across the C-family and Python comment conventions we target,
and we return to its limits in Section~\ref{sec:validity}.

Names occurring only in masked regions are not discarded. They are reported as
informational mentions, excluded from the count and the score. This matters for
usability under goal G1: a reviewer who sees that the tool located
\texttt{ECDSA} in a comment and deliberately declined to count it gains
confidence, whereas silent omission is indistinguishable from failure to look.

\subsection{Per-Line Family Deduplication}
\label{sec:dedup}

Pattern sets of this kind necessarily overlap, because they must accommodate
both qualified and bare invocation forms. The statement
\texttt{hashlib.sha256(payload)} matches both the qualified pattern and the bare
one, and the import \texttt{from ecdsa import SigningKey, SECP256k1} matches
both the import pattern and the curve-name pattern. The naive detector emits two
findings in each case, although the engineer visits one location.

We collapse matches that share an algorithm family and a line number, retaining
the longest matched text on the grounds that it is the most specific description
of the call site. Family is part of the key rather than being collapsed away: a
line that genuinely invokes two different families, such as an ECDSA signature
over a SHA-256 digest, legitimately produces two findings because it requires
two distinct remediations.

This is a correctness fix and not merely cosmetic, since the raw count
propagates into the score, the remediation estimate and any reported totals. On
our benchmark the mechanism reduces 38 raw matches to 26, and the residual
difference between 26 and the 19 labelled findings is accounted for entirely by
the hard-negative artefacts, where the correct count is zero.

\subsection{A Non-Saturating Exposure Score}
\label{sec:scoring}

The preliminary version of this system scored an artefact by summing severity
weights and capping the result at 100, with high findings weighted 30. The
consequence is immediate: four high-severity findings reach 120, so every
artefact containing four or more of them scores exactly 100 regardless of size,
breadth or density. Across the scan records accumulated during development the
mean score was 99.5, with effectively no variance. A metric that assigns the
same value to every input carries no information, and because the score is the
figure that appears on compliance documentation, the defect is consequential
rather than academic.

We replace it with a bounded combination of three signals that vary
independently:

\begin{equation}
S = \min\!\left(100,\;
\underbrace{\max_{f \in F} \sigma(f)}_{\text{severity} \le 60} +
\underbrace{\min(20,\, 5(|A| - 1))}_{\text{breadth} \le 20} +
\underbrace{20\left(1 - e^{-\rho/5}\right)}_{\text{density} \le 20}
\right)
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $F$ is the set of findings, $\sigma$ maps severity to a base
value ($\text{HIGH} \mapsto 60$, $\text{MEDIUM} \mapsto 35$,
$\text{LOW} \mapsto 15$), $A$ is the set of distinct families present, and
$\rho = 100|F| / L$ is findings per hundred lines for an artefact of $L$ lines.

The three terms answer different questions: whether anything is outright broken,
how widely the exposure is distributed, and how concentrated it is. The density
term uses an exponential with diminishing returns so that a dense artefact is
distinguished from a sparse one without a single pathological file dominating.
The score is monotonic in all three inputs and reaches 100 only for an artefact
that is both broadly and densely affected. We make no claim that the weights are
optimal; they are chosen so the score is interpretable and discriminating, and
Section~\ref{sec:validity} notes that their calibration against expert judgement
remains open.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

\subsection{Reference Implementation}

The reference implementation is Python, exposed through a FastAPI service of
twelve endpoints covering scanning, migration planning, report generation,
inventory export, certificate issuance and ledger anchoring. Analysis state is
file-backed rather than held in a database, which keeps deployment minimal at
the cost of concurrent-access guarantees; we regard this as appropriate for an
assessment tool invoked per artefact.

Two deployment faults found during hardening are worth recording, since both are
common in research prototypes. The ledger client was constructed at import time,
so an absent signing key prevented the entire service from starting rather than
disabling one feature; it is now constructed lazily and only the anchoring
endpoints degrade. And the anchoring endpoint, which spends funds from a
server-held key, was initially unauthenticated, making it an open relay against
the operator's own balance. The architecture of Section~\ref{sec:anchor}
eliminates the underlying hazard rather than guarding it.

\subsection{Dual Runtime and Verified Equivalence}
\label{sec:dualruntime}

Goal G3 requires that an organisation be able to assess proprietary ledger code
without transmitting it. Since detection is lexical, it has no server-side
dependency, so we additionally implemented the full detection path in
JavaScript. The browser runtime reads files through the local file API, performs
masking, matching, deduplication and scoring in the page, and renders the
reports and inventory client-side. No source leaves the machine.

Maintaining two implementations of the same method invites divergence, which
would be a serious defect: the evaluated implementation and the deployed one
would differ. We therefore treat equivalence as a verified property. A harness
executes the JavaScript detection module outside the browser and compares its
output against the Python implementation across the full benchmark on six
fields: the finding set as (line, family) pairs, the finding count, the
informational-mention set, the detected post-quantum algorithms, the exposure
score and the line count. All seven artefacts agree on all six fields. The check
is mechanical and is intended to be run whenever the pattern set changes.

\subsection{Cryptographic Bill of Materials}
\label{sec:cbom}

Each assessment exports a CycloneDX 1.6~\cite{cyclonedx} document in which every
distinct family present becomes one \texttt{cryptographic-asset} component
carrying its primitive class, severity, occurrence count, affected lines,
recommended replacement and governing standard. Using the standard schema rather
than a bespoke report format allows the output to enter existing supply-chain
review processes, and allows an organisation to track cryptographic exposure
across releases with tools it already operates.

\subsection{Non-Custodial Ledger Anchoring}
\label{sec:anchor}

Goal G4 requires that an assessment be independently verifiable. We serialise
the assessment metadata --- identifier, organisation, timestamp, finding count
and score --- compute its Keccak-256 digest, and record the digest through a
registry contract on the Ethereum Sepolia testnet. The ledger supplies ordering
and tamper evidence: a party holding the assessment can recompute the digest and
confirm both that it matches and when it was recorded, without trusting the
assessor. Only the digest is published, so no source material is disclosed.

The signing arrangement matters more than it may appear. A server-side key, as
in our preliminary version, makes the operator a custodian: the key must be
funded, protected and rotated, and any endpoint that uses it is a potential
drain on the operator's balance. We instead have the transaction signed by the
assessing party's own wallet in the browser. The service holds no key, there is
nothing to drain, and the resulting record is attributable to the party that
actually performed the assessment, which is a stronger provenance property than
a shared service key provides.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Benchmark Construction}
\label{sec:benchmark}

We constructed a labelled benchmark of seven source artefacts. A label marks an
\emph{active call site} of a quantum-vulnerable primitive, recorded as a (line,
family) pair; a prediction counts as a true positive only when both components
match. This is deliberately strict, because a finding that names the right
family on the wrong line does not tell an engineer where to work.

The labelling policy is fixed in advance and released with the data. Import
statements are labelled, since an import of a vulnerable primitive is part of
the migration surface. Call sites are labelled. A function definition naming a
primitive is labelled when its body implements that primitive. Occurrences in
comments, documentation strings and string literals are not labelled. Nor are
identifiers that merely contain a primitive name as a substring.

Four artefacts are positive cases: a Solidity consent registry using
\texttt{ecrecover} and \texttt{keccak256}; a Python settlement signer combining
ECDSA, RSA, SHA-256, short-key AES and a Diffie--Hellman exchange; a Python key
service; and a hybrid Solidity contract in which an ML-DSA path has been added
while the legacy \texttt{ecrecover} path remains live. The hybrid case tests
that a partially migrated artefact still reports its residual classical
exposure, which is the common real-world state.

Three artefacts carry zero labels and constitute the hard-negative component.
The first is a fully post-quantum module importing \texttt{dilithium2} and
\texttt{kyber512} and hashing with SHA-384. The second documents a completed
migration in prose, naming \texttt{RSA.generate}, \texttt{hashlib.sha256},
\texttt{ECDSA.sign}, \texttt{Diffie-Hellman} and \texttt{ecrecover} across
comments, a documentation string and a literal, while invoking none of them. The
third contains identifiers such as \texttt{bandwidth},
\texttt{sha256\_cache\_enabled}, \texttt{rsa\_config\_path} and
\texttt{ecdsa\_feature\_flag}, none of which is cryptographic. Each negative
targets one of the three error classes discussed in Section~\ref{sec:method}.
The benchmark totals nineteen labelled findings.

\subsection{Ablation Design}

We score three configurations to attribute the improvement to specific
mechanisms:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{v1-baseline}: the preliminary pattern set, matched against raw
  text, without deduplication. This is a real prior configuration of this
  system, retained verbatim, not a reconstruction.
  \item \textbf{v2-boundaries}: the revised, word-anchored pattern set, still
  matched against raw text without deduplication.
  \item \textbf{v3-full}: the revised pattern set with offset-preserving masking
  and per-line deduplication. This is the shipped configuration.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the ablation. Three observations follow.

First, recall is unaffected throughout. The precision mechanisms do not suppress
genuine call sites, which is the property that must hold for them to be
acceptable --- a precision gain purchased with recall would be a poor trade in a
security inventory.

Second, word anchoring alone does not improve precision on this benchmark. The
v2 configuration removes one raw match relative to v1 but eliminates no false
positive. This is informative: the substring-identifier error class that
anchoring targets is real, and our false-friend artefact was written to exercise
it, but on this data the dominant error source is comment and string matching,
which anchoring does not address. Reporting this negative result matters, since
boundary anchoring is the intuitive first remedy and is insufficient alone.

Third, masking together with deduplication eliminates all eleven false
positives. The eleven comprise matches inside comments and documentation strings
in both positive and negative artefacts, and duplicate matches on shared call
sites. Per-family results are uniform at 1.000 precision and recall across
ECDSA (4 findings), RSA (4), SHA-256 (8), Diffie--Hellman (2) and short-key AES
(1).

\paragraph{What these numbers do and do not establish.} The absolute scores must
not be read as a field accuracy estimate, and we state the reason plainly. The
benchmark is small, at seven artefacts and nineteen findings, and it was
authored alongside the detector by its developers. Under those conditions
perfect scores on the final configuration are close to a construction artefact:
the negatives were written to exercise error classes we had already decided to
address. What the table does establish is narrower and still useful --- that
each mechanism removes the error class it targets, that the removal is complete
on data designed to be adversarial for a lexical detector, and that recall does
not pay for it. The appropriate reading is the \emph{delta}, from 0.633 to
1.000, under an ablation that isolates the cause. An independent estimate
requires a corpus the detector's authors did not construct, which we identify as
the primary item of future work in Section~\ref{sec:future}.

Sample size compounds the point. Nineteen labelled positives support a Wilson
95\% interval on precision of only $[0.832, 1.000]$ even when every one is
correct, and the per-family figures are weaker still: the single short-key AES
finding yields $[0.207, 1.000]$, which is to say nothing at all about that
family. We therefore do not claim a precision level from this benchmark, only a
difference between configurations measured on the same data. Reaching an
interval tight enough to support a claim above 0.95 requires on the order of
150 labelled positives, which sets the scale of the corpus described in
Section~\ref{sec:future}.

Table~\ref{tab:scores} contrasts the two scoring models across the whole
benchmark. Saturation appears exactly where the analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:scoring} predicts: the two artefacts whose weighted severity
sum exceeds the cap both receive 100.0, and the model therefore cannot
distinguish a 9-finding, 108-line artefact from a 5-finding, 26-line one even
though the second is more than twice as densely affected. The 19 scan records
accumulated during development show the same effect at greater scale, with a
mean of 99.5 and almost no variance, because artefacts drawn from real
deployments routinely exceed four high-severity findings. The three-term model
assigns four distinct values to the four vulnerable artefacts and preserves zero
across all three negatives.

Two properties of the new model deserve comment rather than concealment. First,
on small artefacts it can return a \emph{higher} score than the severity sum:
the hybrid contract rises from 45.0 to 79.3 because two live high-severity
families in 32 lines is genuinely dense exposure, which a count-based sum
understates. Second, the ordering is not by finding count. The key service
scores above the settlement signer despite having fewer findings, because the
density term dominates at 19.2 findings per hundred lines against 8.3. This is
the intended behaviour --- a small file that is almost entirely vulnerable
warrants more urgency than a large file with scattered call sites --- but it
means the score must be read as exposure concentration rather than as a count
proxy.

We emphasise that this demonstrates \emph{discrimination}, not \emph{validity}.
We show the new model separates artefacts the old one could not, and that its
behaviour follows from stated terms rather than from a cap artefact. We have not
established that its ordering matches expert risk judgement, which would require
a ranking study we have not conducted and which we identify in
Section~\ref{sec:future}.

\subsection{Runtime Equivalence}

The equivalence harness reports agreement between the Python and JavaScript
implementations on all seven artefacts across all six compared fields. The
deployed browser runtime therefore carries the measured behaviour of the
evaluated reference implementation rather than an approximation of it. We regard
this as a necessary condition for reporting evaluation results obtained from one
implementation while deploying another.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Regulatory Alignment}

The outputs map onto obligations that regulated Indian entities already carry.
The RBI cybersecurity framework requires regulated entities to maintain
awareness of cryptographic exposure and to document remediation; CERT-In
directions impose incident and asset reporting duties for which a cryptographic
inventory is a prerequisite. Internationally, NIST's transition
guidance~\cite{nistir8547} sets deprecation dates against which an organisation
must be able to demonstrate progress, and ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A controls on
cryptography require a documented policy and its evidence.

The combination of a dated, tamper-evident digest and a standards-conformant
inventory addresses a specific difficulty in this setting: demonstrating
\emph{when} an assessment was performed to a party that was not present. The
ledger record provides that without requiring the verifier to trust the
assessor, and without disclosing the assessed source.

\subsection{Sector Applicability}

The exposure profile differs by sector in ways that affect prioritisation.
Pharmaceutical and manufacturing deployments typically use ledgers for batch
provenance, where signatures authenticate custody transfer; a forged historical
signature corrupts a provenance chain that regulators rely on. Healthcare
deployments anchor consent and record integrity, where the retroactive threat is
acute because the confidentiality requirement extends over a patient's lifetime
and beyond. Financial deployments carry the shortest secrecy lifetimes but the
largest immediate value at risk, and the most explicit supervisory expectations.
Under Mosca's inequality~\cite{mosca2018} the first two categories are more
urgent than their current attention would suggest, since their secrecy lifetimes
are the longest.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:validity}

We separate limitations of the method from limitations of the evaluation.

\paragraph{Method.} Detection is lexical. It identifies cryptographic API usage
and cannot determine whether a correctly named primitive is used correctly, nor
follow indirection: a project that wraps ECDSA behind a generic
\texttt{sign()} facade in one module and calls that facade elsewhere yields one
finding at the wrapper, not at every caller. For migration planning this is
often the desired behaviour, since the wrapper is the correct place to change,
but it understates the blast radius. Dynamically constructed algorithm
selection, dependency-supplied cryptography, and compiled artefacts are all
outside scope. Masking is lexical approximation rather than parsing, so
constructs such as nested or unconventional string delimiters can mask
imprecisely; we bound the damage by forbidding single-line literal patterns from
crossing newlines, but a parser-based implementation per language would be
strictly more accurate.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Three threats to validity apply, in decreasing order of
seriousness. \emph{Construct validity}: the benchmark was authored by the
detector's own developers, so the negatives test error classes we had already
chosen to address, and the v3 scores are correspondingly optimistic. This is the
dominant limitation of the present evaluation and we do not minimise it.
\emph{External validity}: seven artefacts of a few dozen lines each cannot
represent production ledger codebases, which are larger, use more libraries, and
contain idioms we have not anticipated. \emph{Internal validity}: the exact
(line, family) matching criterion is strict and defensible, but a tool reporting
a finding one line from the label is scored as both a false positive and a false
negative, which penalises near-misses more heavily than an engineer would.

\paragraph{Scope of the artefacts.} The ledger anchoring uses a testnet, so the
records carry no economic finality; a production deployment would use a mainnet
or a permissioned ledger. Certificate issuance is backed by a local registry
rather than a public verification service, so a certificate is currently
evidence of an assessment rather than an independently resolvable credential.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}

A tool that locates cryptographic weakness is dual-use, and we considered this
in its design. The exposure it reports is not secret: the vulnerability of ECDSA
to Shor's algorithm is public, and the public keys at issue are already on
public ledgers, so an adversary gains no information from our output that they
could not obtain directly. The asymmetry favours defenders, who must find every
site, over attackers, who need only one. We therefore judge publication net
beneficial. Two design decisions follow from the same reasoning: the browser
runtime means an organisation need not disclose source to a third party to be
assessed, and only digests, never source or findings, are published to the
ledger. We also consider it an ethical requirement that the tool not overstate
its own assurance, which is why certificate issuance is refused while any
finding remains and why the reports carry the method's limitations in the
document itself.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

The clear priority is an independent evaluation corpus, and we have implemented
and released the harness for it so that the protocol is fixed before any data is
collected rather than chosen afterwards. It operates in three stages, with the
separation between them doing the methodological work.

Stage one draws contracts from creation transactions in randomly chosen blocks
within a recent range, retains those with verified source, and generates
\emph{candidate lines} for human adjudication. Candidate generation is
deliberately cruder than the detector --- bare case-insensitive substrings, no
word boundaries, no masking, no deduplication --- so that the candidate set is a
strict superset of anything the detector can report. This is what makes detector
false negatives measurable: a call site the detector misses is still presented to
the annotator. Stage three verifies the superset property holds and raises a
harness error if any detector finding falls outside the candidate set.

Stage two presents each candidate with surrounding context and records what the
line actually invokes. The annotation tool does not import the detector and
never displays its prediction, because an annotator shown a prediction produces
labels that inherit the detector's biases, and the resulting accuracy figure
would be circular. Two annotators label independently, and disagreements are
excluded from the primary score rather than resolved by majority vote, which
would manufacture confidence the data does not support.

Stage three reports Cohen's $\kappa$ between annotators alongside precision,
recall and $F_1$ per family with Wilson intervals. We treat $\kappa$ as a gate
rather than as a statistic to report in passing: a value below roughly 0.6 would
indicate that our labelling policy is ambiguous, in which case the policy must be
revised and the corpus relabelled before any accuracy figure is quoted.

Three further directions follow. Replacing lexical masking with per-language
parsing would eliminate the approximation discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:validity} and would permit call-graph analysis, so that
indirection through wrappers could be resolved and the true blast radius
reported. Calibrating the score weights of Eq.~\ref{eq:score} against expert
risk rankings would move the model from discriminating to validated. Finally,
measuring the gas cost of on-chain lattice verification under current and
proposed precompiles would establish when the architectural workaround of
Section~\ref{sec:background} can be retired in favour of direct verification.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Organisations holding blockchain deployments face a migration whose first step is
an inventory, and the tooling they have does not produce one: smart-contract
analysers report logical defects, and cryptographic-misuse detectors report
incorrect use of sound primitives, whereas the problem here is correct use of
primitives that will become unsound. We presented a toolkit that produces that
inventory and carries it through to migration planning, standards-conformant
export, regulator-facing documentation and verifiable records.

Our substantive finding concerns precision. A lexical detector for this task is
easy to make complete and hard to make precise, and its errors are dominated by
two systematic classes rather than by scattered noise: matches inside comments
and string literals, and multiple patterns firing on one call site.
Offset-preserving masking and per-line family deduplication remove both, raising
precision from 0.633 to 1.000 at unchanged recall on a benchmark built to be
adversarial for this class of detector, while word anchoring alone --- the
intuitive first remedy --- removes no false positive at all. We also showed that
the severity-sum scoring common in tools of this kind saturates, assigning an
identical maximum to artefacts differing severalfold in extent, and gave a
three-term alternative that discriminates between them.

We are deliberate about what we have not shown. Because the benchmark was
authored alongside the detector, the absolute figures are optimistic and the
defensible result is the ablation delta rather than the endpoint. Establishing
field accuracy requires an independently labelled corpus, which is where we
direct our next effort. The implementation, the benchmark, the labelling policy
and the evaluation harness are released so that both the result and its
limitations can be checked.
